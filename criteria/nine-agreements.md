# The Nine Agreements

People say yes to an offer only after they have agreed to nine things **in order**.
When selling remotely without a sales call, the material has to answer each
challenge before the prospect raises it. Each agreement builds on the one before,
so assess them in sequence and note where the chain breaks.

## Scoring scale (use for every agreement)

| Score | Label   | Meaning |
|-------|---------|---------|
| 0     | Missing | Nothing in the material addresses this agreement. |
| 1     | Weak    | Hinted at, generic or marketing-speak; no specifics, numbers or story. |
| 2     | Present | Clearly addressed with some specifics, but the proof or emotion is thin. |
| 3     | Strong  | Specific, concrete, in the customer's own words, backed by numbers or a real story. |

**Rule of thumb from the worksheet:** if an agreement can't be shown with a
specific example or number from the material, it is a gap (score 0–1).

---

## 1. "This problem is serious enough to fix now."
The customer has to feel the problem costing them something today, and see that
doing nothing is also a choice.

- **Look for:** a stated cost of the problem (money, time, stress) per month/year; what happens if they wait another year.
- **Strong looks like:** "It costs about £400 a month in missed sales." A concrete picture of next year if nothing changes.
- **Red flags:** problem described in abstract terms ("struggling to grow"); no cost at all; jumps straight to the solution.
- **Worksheet questions:** (a) What is the problem costing your customers each month, in money, time or stress? (b) What happens if they wait another year?

## 2. "What I've been doing can't work."
They must see that their current approach is the flaw, not that they are failing at it.

- **Look for:** naming what people usually try first; explaining why all of those fall short for the *same underlying reason*.
- **Strong looks like:** "You've tried X, Y and Z. They all fail because…"
- **Red flags:** attacking the customer's effort; no mention of alternatives; implying they just need to try harder.
- **Worksheet questions:** (a) What do people usually try first? (b) Why does each of those fall short for the same underlying reason?

## 3. "The reason is something I didn't know."
Reveal the hidden cause, and let them off the hook for past failures.

- **Look for:** a named, non-obvious root cause; explicit reassurance that past failure wasn't their fault.
- **Strong looks like:** a genuine "aha" insight most competitors don't say.
- **Red flags:** the "cause" is just the absence of the product; blame-y tone; no new insight.
- **Worksheet questions:** (a) What is the real cause that most people miss? (b) What can you say so they stop blaming themselves?

## 4. "This person understands me."
Describe their situation more accurately than they could themselves, so they trust you with the fix.

- **Look for:** a scene or story of the moment the problem hits; the thoughts they never say out loud; one clear typical customer.
- **Strong looks like:** the reader thinks "that's exactly me". Specific, sensory, written the way people speak.
- **Red flags:** written for everyone; company-centric ("we are passionate about…"); jargon; no scene.
- **Worksheet questions:** (a) Describe the moment the problem hits (as a scene or story). (b) What do they think but never say out loud?

## 5. "There's a genuinely different solution."
One named method that is clearly not a better version of what has already let them down.

- **Look for:** a named method/system/process, explainable in one sentence; how it differs from what they've tried; something they can picture (steps, diagram, framework).
- **Strong looks like:** "The [Name] Method: three steps…" clearly contrasted with agreement 2's failed approaches.
- **Red flags:** a list of services instead of a method; "better/faster/cheaper" version of the old thing; unnamed or vague process.
- **Worksheet questions:** (a) What is your method called, in one sentence? (b) How is it different from what they've tried? (c) Can they picture it?

## 6. "This solution is proven."
Show results from several angles, ideally ones so strong even the most sceptical reader would believe.

- **Look for:** results with evidence (numbers, screenshots, named clients, logos, data, press, credentials); variety of proof types; claims a sceptic might doubt and whether they are backed.
- **Strong looks like:** multiple proof types, specific numbers, verifiable names.
- **Red flags:** unbacked claims ("industry-leading"); anonymous testimonials; only one type of proof; results with no context.
- **Worksheet questions:** (a) What results can you show, with evidence? (b) Which claims might a sceptic doubt, and how do you back them up or drop them?

## 7. "I can see myself succeeding."
Proof has to feel real, not just numbers and logic. They need to see people like them, starting where they start, get the result.

- **Look for:** at least one full client story, step by step, from a weak starting position; relatable case studies; objection handling ("this isn't for me because…").
- **Strong looks like:** a before → during → after story of someone the reader identifies with; "even if you…" objection handling.
- **Red flags:** only star clients or big brands; testimonials that praise the provider but don't describe the journey; no objection handling.
- **Worksheet questions:** (a) Who is your best example of someone who started from a weak position? (b) What happened, step by step? (c) What do people say about why it isn't for them?

## 8. "Doing nothing costs more than acting."
Put the price next to the cost of the problem, ideally with numbers they work out themselves.

- **Look for:** price (or price range) shown or implied; an explicit comparison with the cost of the problem from agreement 1; a calculator or "do the maths" prompt.
- **Strong looks like:** "The problem costs you £4,800 a year. This costs £1,200."
- **Red flags:** no price context at all; price shown with no value framing; the cost of the problem was never established (agreement 1 gap).
- **Worksheet questions:** (a) What does the problem cost per month or year? (b) How does that compare with your price?

## 9. "I need to act now."
Give a believable reason why now is better than later, and don't contradict it.

- **Look for:** a deadline or constraint with a real reason (limited places, price change, start date); what changes after it passes; a clear call to action.
- **Strong looks like:** "Doors close Friday because the cohort starts Monday and is capped at 12."
- **Red flags:** no reason to act now; fake/evergreen countdowns; urgency contradicted elsewhere (e.g. "book anytime"); weak or multiple competing CTAs.
- **Worksheet questions:** (a) What is the deadline and the real reason for it? (b) What changes after it passes?

## Cross-checks (run after scoring all nine)

1. **Sequence:** does the material move through the agreements roughly in order, or does it pitch (5, 8, 9) before establishing the problem (1–4)? Where does the chain break first?
2. **Missing proof (6 and 7):** the most common gap. Flag if there is no full client story.
3. **Vague cost of waiting (1 and 8):** makes the offer feel optional. Flag if no real number appears.
4. **Deadline with no reason (9):** will be ignored. Flag if urgency has no true constraint.
5. **Voice:** is it written the way the owner would speak to one typical customer, or in marketing phrases?
